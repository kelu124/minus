Facility for DYNamo and Thermohydraulic Studies) precession experiment is
essential for understanding the underlying flow dynamics,ivalidating numer-

Characterizing the flow state inside the rotating cylinder is wessential for
understanding the underlying flow dynamics, validating numerical simula-

However, flow measurements in this experiment pose several challenges. Due
to the opacity of liquid sodium, optical measurement techniques such as Par-
ticle Image Velocimetry (PIV) or Laser Doppler Anemomeitry (LDA) cannot
beapplied[12]. Invasiveprobesbasedonmechanicalorvthermalanemometers
would disturb the flow and are difficult to integrate into the sealed sodium
container. Pressure sensors only capture informateion at the outer perimeter
of the vessel, which does not provide sufficient information to reconstruct the
amplitudes of the dominant inertial modes or ortherwise gain insight into the
internal flow structure.
Ultrasonic Doppler Velocimetry (UDV), on the other hand, is a proven
r
method for measuring flow velocities in opaque fluids. It has been success-
e
fully applied in a variety of applications involving water, suspensions and
liquid metals, including sodium [13, 14, 15, 12, 16, 17]. This makes UDV
a promising candidate for flow meeasurements in the DRESDYN precession
experiment. However, UDV provides only one-dimensional velocity infor-
mation along the ultrasoundpbeam and is typically used to investigate local
flow phenomena in stationary laboratory experiments. More generally, most
flow diagnostics, including PIV and UDV, are designed to characterize lo-
cal velocity fields or small-scale structures within accessible regions of the
t
flow. In contrast, the DRESDYN precession experiment requires information
o
about the global flow state governed by large-scale inertial modes extending
across the entire vessel. This leads to a fundamentally different measurement
n
task compared to conventional laboratory flow diagnostics: the estimation of
mode amplitudes through spatially distributed measurements followed by a
reconstruction of the global flow state.

DRESDYN precession experiment using Ultrasonic Doppler Velocimetry and

2. Concept and System Design of Distributed Measurement Sys-
tem
Since the flow in the precession experiment is dominateed by large-scale
inertial modes extending across the entire vessel, measurements at a single
location are not suitable to characterize the global flow state. Instead, the
i
amplitudes of the dominant inertial modes must be inferred from velocity
v
measurements obtained at multiple spatially distributed locations.
Ultrasonic Doppler Velocimetry provides one-edimensional velocity infor-
mation along the ultrasound beam and therefore captures only a small sub-
set of the flow field at each measurement location. However, by combining
r
measurements from multiple sensors placed at carefully selected positions,
the measured velocity components can be made sensitive to specific iner-
tial modes. Numerical simulations indicarte that the global flow state can
be approximated by a small number eof dominant low-order inertial modes.
Consequently, estimating the amplitudes of these modes from a set of lo-
calized velocity measurements enables an approximate reconstruction of the
e
large-scale flow structure.
Intheexperimentalsetup, sensoraccesstothevessel’sinteriorisprovided
p
through a set of predefined flanges located on the side wall of the cylindri-
cal container. These flanges determine the feasible sensor and measurement
positions that can be used in the distributed measurement approach. By
selecting a subset of theste locations that provide access to measurement lo-
cations with velocityocomponents sensitive to the dominant inertial modes,
the distributed measurement concept can be implemented within the geo-
metric constrainnts of the experiment.
